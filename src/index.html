The weight of being born here

A journey through data to understand how
place of birth shapes the future of millions of children.

Every year, millions of children are born into conditions that compromise their life chances. Income, access to education, and state presence are the variables that determine whether a child will go to school or go to work, whether they will marry at thirteen, or whether they will one day have to leave their homeland.

This story is not about abstract statistics. It is about real lives, denied rights, and a global divide that cannot be ignored. We follow a four-step journey: from economic and social fragility, to missed schooling, to the harshest consequences of poverty, and finally to flight as the last response to a system that has failed.

Chapter I

The foundations of inequality

It is not only about how much a country earns, but how unequal it is.
Behind every national average lies a brutal truth: a country's wealth is not the wealth of its people.

The geography of wealth

GDP per capita is one of the most immediate indicators for measuring the average economic well-being of a population.
Looking at the global map, the divide is clear: while European countries show values measured in tens of thousands of dollars, much of the African continent rarely rises above five thousand.
This is not a static snapshot, but the result of decades of policies, conflicts, and structural inequalities that have accumulated over time.

A gap that does not close

Looking at the trend over time, Europe experienced steady growth in GDP per capita over the last years, while Africa recorded slow growth, almost negligible.
What matters most here is not the change itself, because Europe was already ahead in 2000 and it is no surprise that it is still ahead in 2023, but the persistence of a gap that does not shrink and instead it is widening more and more.

Where poverty does not recede

Within the African continent, the differences are far from negligible. Countries such as South Africa, Botswana, Libya, and Egypt show relatively higher values, reflecting more diversified economies.
However, most countries in Sub-Saharan Africa, especially in the Sahel and in Central and Eastern Africa, remain among the poorest in the world. For tens of millions of people, GDP per capita is not an abstract indicator: it directly reflects what they can afford each day, including whether their children go to school or to work.

Income and well-being: an overview

Each bubble is a country. The correlation is clear: the farther right you move, the higher you go. This means that higher income is associated with higher life expectancy. This is not surprising, since life expectancy at birth is perhaps the most human indicator among those available: with a single number it reflects health system quality, access to food, safe water, and basic care.
The gap between Africa and Europe tells two deeply different development stories, and the distance between the two curves is measured in decades.

A fifteen-year gap

At the global level, life expectancy increased significantly over the twentieth century, but this progress was not distributed evenly.
Europe saw life expectancy rise steadily in the second half of the twentieth century thanks to investment in health systems, prevention campaigns, and dignified living conditions. European bubbles, already high and to the right in 2000, move only slightly along the slider: not because the system stopped improving, but because it had already approached its practical limit.
In Africa, the path was much more difficult: the HIV/AIDS epidemic in the 1990s devastated entire generations in countries such as Zimbabwe and Lesotho. Only from the 2000s onward, thanks to antiretroviral access programs and investment in primary care, did life expectancy start rising again.
Yet the gap with Europe still exceeds fifteen years. Fifteen years of life that depend on where you are born.

Multidimensional Poverty Index

GDP alone is not enough to describe poverty. For this reason, the United Nations Development Programme developed the Multidimensional Poverty Index (MPI), which measures simultaneous deprivation across three core dimensions: health, education, and living standards, through ten indicators ranging from child mortality and access to safe water to electricity and years of schooling.
A person is considered multidimensionally poor if they are deprived in at least one third of the ten indicators considered.
It is a more complex and more truthful measure of human well-being: it does not only ask how much money you have, but how you actually live.

The most severe cases: beyond the 0.30 threshold

A country can grow economically and still leave a large share of its population without essential services.
The distribution of countries by MPI shows that a large share of African countries lies beyond the 0.3 threshold, which the UNDP defines as severe poverty.
Countries such as Niger, Chad, and the Central African Republic show MPI values above 0.45: this means deprivation affects an absolute majority of the population across multiple dimensions at the same time.
These are not marginal numbers. They are the norm for people born in those regions.

The geography of deprivation

When we move to the map view, the geographic dimension of the problem becomes clear.
The most affected areas are the Sahel and Central Africa.
Nothing here is accidental: this distribution reflects centuries of colonial exploitation, political instability, state fragility, and marginalization.
The map shows precisely where the problem is most urgent.

Chapter II

School as a luxury for the few

Education should remove existing social and economic inequalities, offering opportunities regardless of where a child is born. It does not.
Where money is scarce, school excludes: overcrowded classrooms, absent teachers, and children who never enter school.

The misleading percentage

Looking at average public spending on education as a share of GDP, the gap between Africa and Europe appears surprisingly limited. Both continents fluctuate around similar values over time, with a difference that stays below two percentage points.
The time trend also does not suggest abandonment: African governments do not appear to ignore education, at least on paper. The percentage alone tells an almost reassuring story.

The real value

Once we move to total absolute USD, the picture changes completely. The same percentage applied to very different GDP levels produces amounts that are not even comparable: European countries invest in a single student what, in many African countries, corresponds to the entire annual school budget.
The percentage measures intent, while the absolute value measures reality: in terms of public investment, an African child receives an infinitesimal fraction of what a European child receives.

Spending more is not enough

Not all school systems respond in the same way to higher spending, so it is necessary to look beyond the surface and examine the actual outcomes of the African education system. We want to answer one question: how much difference do these investments really make?
To answer it, we relate public spending on education to two key outcomes: youth literacy in secondary school and exclusion from primary school. The curve connects the points year by year and shows that the relationship between resources and results is never linear.

Uncertain progress

The literacy curve against spending shows a complex path.
The overall trend is positive: the dashed trend line rises from left to right, indicating that over the long term more spending is associated with higher literacy, but the curve loops back on itself several times, telling a more complex story.
Only in the latest years, with spending above 20 billion dollars, does the curve stabilize around 80-85% and stop oscillating. Real progress, built on twenty years of uncertainty.

Classrooms that remain empty

The curve for children out of primary school tells a similar story. The overall trend improves, with the dashed line moving downward from left to right: as spending increases, the share of excluded children tends to fall.
But here too the curve is never linear: in recent years a new and worrying rise in out-of-school children has appeared.
Money alone is not enough: without infrastructure, teachers, and families that can afford to send children to school instead of to work, the investment is lost.

Gender Parity Index

The UNESCO Institute for Statistics developed the Gender Parity Index (GPI), which measures gender parity in participation and educational opportunity for women relative to men. It is calculated as the ratio between the number of girls and the number of boys enrolled at a given level of education.
A value of 1 indicates parity. A value below 1 generally indicates disadvantage for girls, while a value above 1 indicates disadvantage for boys. The closer the GPI is to one, the closer a country is to equal access to education for males and females. UNESCO considers a country to have reached gender parity when its GPI lies between 0.97 and 1.03.
It is a simple but powerful indicator: it summarizes in one number how much an education system guarantees equal access regardless of sex. It also reflects the broader position of women in society.

Europe: parity achieved

Looking closely at European countries, the bars cluster almost entirely around the parity line, with girls attending secondary school at the same rate as boys.
This is not a trivial result: it reflects decades of active policy, enforced school obligations, and a cultural context that does not penalize female education.

Africa: a system that does not retain children

In Africa the internal spread is wide, and a country-by-country view reveals two opposite dynamics hidden by the continental average.
On one side, countries such as South Sudan, Chad, and Mali show very low GPI values: here it is girls who are excluded, often because of child marriage, imposed domestic work, and excessive distance from school.
On the other side, in some countries of Eastern and Southern Africa the GPI is above 1: here boys leave earlier, often pushed into informal and domestic labor, into mines or fields when the family faces severe economic hardship, or even forced to take up arms.
Two different consequences of a single failure: an education system unable to keep children in school when family economic survival is at stake.

Chapter III

Stolen childhood

Children are the ones who pay the price for poverty and denied education.
These are not just difficult conditions, they are the breeding ground for the most serious violations of children’s rights.
Child labor, child marriage, and female genital mutilation are not isolated or inevitable practices. They are direct consequences of failing systems.

The poorer the country, the younger the workers

The chart divides African countries into four quadrants. Most observations cluster in the top-left quadrant (low income, high child labor) and the bottom-right quadrant (high income, low child labor), almost forming two separate clusters.
The correlation is clear: the lower the GDP per capita, the higher the share of children aged 5 to 17 forced to work.
The African continent concentrates most of the critical cases. Countries such as Burundi, Niger, Chad, and Madagascar are among the poor countries with the highest child labor rates in the world, showing an economic system that still fails to protect childhood.

A cycle children cannot escape

Child labor is not a cultural choice. It is the response of families to poverty when no alternatives exist. When a family cannot survive without the contribution of all its members and the school system is inaccessible, distant, or does not guarantee economic returns, children stop being children and become workers.
It is a vicious cycle: weak education and state economic fragility reinforce each other. Less school means fewer future opportunities and greater dependence on immediate work, while greater dependence on immediate work means fewer children in school and no chance to develop the school system, repeating the same cycle indefinitely.
The chart does not show only a correlation. It shows the mechanism through which poverty spreads.

Africa: one in four girls marries before 18

In Europe the share of marriages under 18 is very low, in many countries even below 1%, while elsewhere it remains limited to very specific contexts. In Africa the continental average is around 24%: nearly one in four girls marries before adulthood. Among them, one in three is younger than 15.
The number alone should be shocking, but the continental average hides the real scale of the problem.

The countries where one girl in two does not choose

When the view expands to the country level, 24% no longer seems so abstract. Chad and the Central African Republic stand out as extreme cases, with 61% of women aged 20 to 24 married before 18. Even more worrying is that these are not isolated anomalies: Mali, Burkina Faso, and Ethiopia are other countries where the rate exceeds 50%.
In absolute terms the numbers become even heavier: we are talking about 126 million girls each year. Countries with relatively lower percentages still contribute significantly to the total because of their population size. Nigeria and Ethiopia alone account for a huge share, with 44 million girls in those two countries forced to marry too early.

A violation of Fundamental Rights

Child marriage is not an immutable tradition. It is the consequence of poverty and of the lack of alternatives for families and for girls, who may see marriage as a source of protection and family honor.
Marriage can place children in adult roles for which they are unprepared, create economic pressure, and limit their opportunities for higher education or professional advancement. It also often compromises a girl's development, causing early pregnancies and social isolation while exposing her to the risk of domestic violence.
Child marriage is a violation of the fundamental rights of minors regardless of sex.
Without a state that protects children's rights, child marriage remains a response to a failing system.

Female Genital Mutilation

Female Genital Mutilation (FGM) is the partial or total removal of the external female genitalia for non-medical reasons. The WHO classifies it as a serious human rights violation, with permanent physical and psychological consequences: chronic pain, childbirth complications, and trauma that girls carry for life. It is most often practiced before age 14, as a rite of passage or a prerequisite for marriage.
The chart breaks down this practice by income quintile, showing how family economic conditions affect the probability that a girl becomes a victim.

Few countries, extremely high numbers

Looking at the map, we can note with relief that FGM is not practiced across the entire African continent: most African countries do not practice it or do so only marginally. But where it is rooted, such as Mauritania, Guinea, Sudan, Mali, and Gambia, the percentages are extremely high, in some cases approaching 80%.
When we expand the view to individual countries, another fact emerges, against the continental average: the practice is not limited to the poorest families. In some countries, such as Mali and Sudan, it affects all income quintiles equally, and in some cases the value even increases in wealthier areas. This indicates that the driving factor is not only economic, but also cultural.

A cultural issue

The reasons behind female genital mutilation are multiple and complex, but they share one feature: in the places where it is practiced, it represents a deeply rooted tradition.
FGM is treated as part of education and as preparation for adulthood and marriage. Many communities consider it a guarantee of virginity and fidelity. In some communities, unmutilated genitals are seen as ugly or impure. In migration contexts, genital mutilation can also serve to preserve a link with the country of origin and maintain cultural identity.
However, where female education is strong and the state is present, FGM declines. Education gives women the tools to recognize and challenge harmful practices, and helps create social networks in which refusal can be expressed without fear of exclusion from the community. By contrast, where girls do not go to school and the state offers no protection, the practice is passed down from one generation to the next. Poverty does not directly cause FGM, but it creates the conditions in which no one has the tools to stop it.

Chapter IV

Escape

When a country cannot protect its citizens, the most logical response is to leave. Migration is the sum of millions of individual decisions that become a collective story.
The final chapter of this story is not about those who stay, but those who leave, and what that says about the place left behind.

The map of escape

How many people born in the African continent live elsewhere today? And where?
In reality, the main destination is still within the continent itself: more than 85% of African migration movements remain within Africa. Extra-continental migration is often driven by conflict, climate disasters, and authoritarian regimes.
Outside Africa, Europe is the most common destination, with France and the United Kingdom concentrating most flows. This pattern reflects old colonial ties, because a shared language and diasporic networks developed after World War II make these destinations more accessible and familiar. Even today, colonialism continues to shape migration flows, but in the opposite direction.
The most relevant fact, however, is not the direction but the number: millions of people have chosen, or been forced, to rebuild their lives elsewhere.
Not all African countries emigrate in the same way. Nigeria, Sudan, and Ethiopia are among the countries with the largest outward flows. This is not a coincidence: they are among the same countries already encountered in previous chapters, with the lowest GDP, the weakest education, the most widespread child marriage, and the most deeply rooted FGM.
Migration is not distributed randomly across space. It follows exactly the map of poverty and violence built chapter by chapter.

The story continues

There is something deeply sad in these numbers. Not because moving is wrong, but because behind every point on the map there is a person who left their language, family, and country not for pleasure, but out of necessity, pushed by years of poverty, denied education, violence, and an absent state.
This story ends here, but what we have described continues every day. Data help us measure the phenomenon, but the responsibility for changing it belongs to us.
